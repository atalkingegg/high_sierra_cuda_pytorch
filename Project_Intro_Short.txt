I have a four-billion-dollar laptop — and so can you!

In 1989, General Atomics spent $25 million to put a Cray Y-MP 8/864 at UCSD’s 
supercomputer center: eight large refrigerators’ worth, consuming 120 kW and 
delivering 2.67 GFLOPs. Twenty-five years later I bought a mid-2014 MacBook Pro 
for $3,100, with a 2.8 GHz Core i7 and NVIDIA GT 750M. Then Apple dropped 
NVIDIA driver support after macOS High Sierra, effectively freezing CUDA on 
that machine in 2017-era software. So, for a slightly ridiculous 
retro-computing project, I decided to see just how far I could drag it into 
modern times: the latest Python 3.14.7, NumPy 2.4.6, retro PyTorch 1.8.1, with 
full GPU-accelerated CUDA 10.1 support. After rebuilding the software stack 
from source and making and applying some code patches, it works!

And that’s where this silly project became amazing. My old MacBook Pro 
benchmarks at about 94 GFLOPs on its CPU plus another 340 GFLOPs on its GPU — 
roughly 162 times (!!) the floating-point performance of that $25 million Cray 
Y-MP. Buying that much computing power in 1989 would have cost well over $4 
billion dollars. Of course, this does not mean you should rush out and buy 
decade-old NVIDIA MacBooks; modern hardware makes even this comparison look 
ridiculous. But if you already have the hardware, enjoy digging through old 
toolchains, and occasionally wonder whether “unsupported” really means 
“impossible,” it turns out a silly retro Python project can unlock a useful 
four-billion-dollar supercomputing laptop!

All the instructions, build scripts, and patches are now up on github.
